ABSTRACT

“For me, a better democracy is a democracy where women do not only have the right to vote and to elect but to be elected.” - Michele Bachelet

It is 2020 and the representation of women in politics is moving at a sluggish pace according to new data presented in the 2020 edition of the IPU‑UN Women map of Women in Politics. This report by NETRI is an attempt to blare the horns and highlight some important lessons for the South Asian countries in terms of women’s representation in politics. Through this endeavour, we have created an overview of women in politics as well as women in ministerial positions across the eight South Asian countries.The report will detail the barriers they face and the respective laws and policies in place to address gender-gaps in politics. A detailed analysis of the efficacy of these laws/frameworks/policies which have worked and what still remains unreformed. We have also attempted to cover the question of whether women politicians govern differently, and observed to some degree their politics-style and policies based on secondary research. The most important objective of this report is to build a repository of knowledge which can be shared across borders, identify areas of intervention and immediate attention and propose coherent initiatives to improve the same.

FIRST BARRIER- ACCESS TO FINANCE

1. How to implement mainstream gender development agendas especially to tackle the issue of financial capital which acts as a barrier for the participation of women in politics?

The first important step would be setting up of regulations that help women candidates in securing campaign finance. However, the long term and sustainable solution would be increased Gender Responsive Budgeting (GRB) to bridge the resource and gender gap.

What is Campaign Finance? Why does it matter?

Campaign finance relates to all the costs associated with the candidate’s political campaign, election related expenditures and maintenance of the party. Funds are needed to establish a campaign office, hire staff, do polling, get the campaign message out and for the candidate to travel and meet the voters. One of the main factors in the increase in the cost of campaigns is the increasing reliance on the use of expensive mass media advertising. Across South Asia, fierce competition, not just in terms of candidates but also in terms of how one is able to raise the most funds, is one of the main criteria of success. Unlike Germany, this process is not supported/ financed by the State to ensure competitive equality or a level playing field and transparency, both essential to a democracy. Competing in an election costs money and is often looked at as an investment. Even after getting elected, the policy decisions are made by the elite members of the party. Therefore, the only incentive for candidates investing heavily in campaigns is to find a way to leverage it and earn more through corrupt means.

Creating positive pressure groups or using politically independent organs of the state in regulating and monitoring campaign finance of the candidates is the first step in achieving equality. In many democracies, campaign finance is highly regulated, a major share of it comes from the party itself or third-party sources like lobbyists and corporate actors, and the personal wealth of the candidate is less important.14 However, such is not the case in many parts of South Asian countries.

The current climate of access to campaign finance affects women in politics disproportionately because it plays heavily on the existing socio-economic divide, creating insurmountable financial barriers. Access to campaign finance is difficult for women owing to majorly three reasons. The first is that political parties are more reluctant to support female candidates owing to existing stereotypes.This reluctance is evident as substantially low funds are delegated for campaigns by women candidates.The prejudice against women is also manifest in the constituencies they are alloted. In many cases women have deliberately been allotted politically unstable or unwinnable constituencies; this practice propagates tokenism in Indian politics. Nadezhda Shvedova describes “an old boys club” atmosphere, which is prejudicial against women and underestimates women’s abilities as candidates (2002) Secondly, female candidates who run as independents have to fund their own campaigns. If the woman is from an affluent political family which actively supports her candidature, it is easier but unfortunately that isn’t the case most of the time.
“The gatekeepers of politics in the end become political parties not only because they can distribute campaign funds, but because they control the nomination process”.15 The nomination process refers to selection of candidates from the party members to contest in elections. In India, a candidate has to submit the following documents to file an election nomination for contesting in Parliament or Assembly elections-

  • Nomination paper: Form 2A for Lok Sabha and Form 2B for Assembly election.

  • Form 26: Affidavit with all the columns completely filled up including details of assets (movable, immovable and foreign assets), pending criminal cases if any, sources of income, educational qualification, liabilities.

  • Form A and B if the candidate is being fielded by a political party.

  • If a candidate belongs to a particular caste then he/she should submit their caste certificate.

  • Electoral roll certified extract.

  • Security Deposit

It is clear from the above list that political parties issue Form A and B to candidates they have nominated from their respective parties. As there are no compliance mechanisms for maintaining internal democracy of the parties, female party members don’t get nominated equally. In this way, the gatekeepers of politics in the end become political parties.

Thirdly, women do not inherit wealth, and if they do- they lack the agency on expenditure and effective control. After so many years of discrimination, it was only this August, 2020 in the case of Sharma v Sharma16 women were recognised as coparceners by the Supreme Court of India. This would mean that daughters would now have inheritance rights equal to that of a son when it comes to ancestral properties in a Joint Hindu Family.

NEPAL

In Nepal, some of the social disadvantages of women are due to early marriage and child-bearing, low access to- health, family planning services, low educational levels and violence against them. This is reinforced by lack of access to and control over productive resources. It has been one of the main hurdles for participation of women in economic activities and the decision-making process.

ROLE OF PROPERTY RIGHTS IN CAMPAIGN FINANCE

Another major issue that affects campaign finance of women politicians is limited property rights for women in the country. Only 5.5 percent households in Nepal have houses in women’s names and only 7.2 percent households reported female livestock ownership. In spite of various credit programs, women’s access to institutional credit remains marginal, both at individual and household enterprise levels irrespective of ecological regions, urban/rural areas and ethnicity/ caste (Acharya 2003). Only 0.8 percent of the households have all three property- house, land, and livestock in female ownership. Almost 83 percent of the households had no property, whatsoever, under women’s legal ownership.17

CHANGING POWER EQUATION USING LAW

The lack of financial resources creates an added insecurity to women trying to enter politics. However, one welcome measure is the legislation that Nepal has in favor of women in terms of property rights. Under the taxation laws, properties registered under the womens’ name have a lesser tax implication and this was a change brought about by the women caucus (which doesn’t exist now). These measures, depending on the geographical location, include a 25 per cent to 50 per cent tax exemption on registration when land is owned by a woman; a 35 per cent tax exemption for single women (Financial Bill 2072, Ministry of Finance); and joint registration of land in the names of husbands and wives with a fee of Rs. 100 (or less than $1)18An important aspect to be observed and learnt from Nepal is their impressive representation of the Dalit community in their government. While most countries are still struggling to ensure representation of marginalised communities in decision making positions, Part 18 of Nepal Constitution guarantees the representation of Dalit women in all local and provincial governments. This has played a crucial role in the participation of Dalit women in governance and politics.

It is pertinent to understand that property rights of women and access to campaign finance are interwoven issues. Access to campaign finance for women politicians in Nepal can be improved if the government intervenes in this process by allocating financial aid to women candidates. Additionally, putting a cap on the expenditure and contribution limits of political parties in general curbs corruption and creates a level playing field.

BHUTAN

In 2008, Bhutan transitioned to a monarchical constitutional democracy from a hereditary monarchy. Since then democracy in Bhutan has matured with each passing election.

It has commendable provisions in place to put a cap on the expenditure and contribution limits.

According to Article 15 (d) and (e) of the Constitution of Bhutan19, political parties should not accept money or any assistance other than those contributions made by its registered members, and the amount or value shall be fixed by the Election Commission; It does not receive money or any assistance from foreign sources, be it governmental, nongovernmental, private organizations or from private parties or individuals;

Another important provision with respect to Public Campaign Finance is Article 1620 that states that, “Parliament shall, by law, establish a Public Election Fund into which shall be paid every year such amounts as the Election Commission may consider appropriate to fund registered political parties and their candidates during elections to the National Assembly and candidates to the National Council:

  1. The payment out of the Public Election Fund shall be made by the Election Commission in a non-discriminatory manner to registered political parties and candidates in accordance with laws made by Parliament.

  2. The Election Commission shall fix a ceiling for the total expenditure that may be incurred by political parties and their candidates taking part in elections to the National Assembly.

  3. The Election Commission shall fix a ceiling for contributions offered voluntarily by any of its registered members to a political party subject to the provisions of the Election Fund Act.

  4. The funding received by political parties and their candidates shall be subjected to scrutiny and auditing as called for by the Election Commission in accordance with laws made by Parliament or law in force.

Bhutan also has the policy of disclosing the finance distributed and used to ensure transparency of campaign process.21All these are welcome measures, but in a patriarchal society, preference is given to male candidates over female candidates and this directly affects their ability to secure funds, forming a vicious cycle. For eg., In 2018, the Bhutan Kuen-Nyam Party (BKP), led by Dasho Neten Zangmo, had the highest number of women candidates—six in total. Unfortunately, the BKP did not manage to get even 10 percent of the votes necessary to be eligible for state funding, and was one of the two parties that lost in the primaries. But the former ruling party, the People’s Democratic Party (PDP), also experienced a shocking loss although they fielded only two women candidates22. Therefore, more measures have to be taken to relax the conditions required to get state funding for women candidates and women led parties to remedy the barriers to women in politics in Bhutan.

MALDIVES

In Maldives, there is a lack of financial resources for contesting in elections. Women in the Maldives, according to the Gender Gap Index, have lower employment rates than men and on average earn half as much as men23. The Maldives electoral system is first past the post [Constitution, section 72 (b)], which, according to expert analysis, works to the disadvantage of women.24 There has been no discussion among political leaders to date in the Maldives about exploring proportional representation or mixed systems to achieve a larger number of female elected officials.
STATE FUNDING AND INCLUSIVITY

State funding is provided for political parties under Section 34 of the Political Parties Act, utilizing a mandatory 0.1 to 0.2 percent of the state budget. The EC distributes these funds to the political parties according to the size of the party’s membership. The EC has not considered proposing an amendment to the law in order to give more money to parties with a greater number of women candidates25 In 2015, an amendment was made to the Political Parties Act, which dissolved parties with membership under 3,000 members, and stipulated that public funding would only be provided for parties with memberships exceeding 10,00026 With this amendment, of the 16 political parties that were registered at the time, 10 were dissolved.27 The minimum requirement for eligibility for public funding also meant that smaller parties faced greater hurdles in conducting their activities. These kinds of practices severely limit the scope of political pluralism and entry of women into the political and decision making spheres. Opposition political parties have since complained that this has taken a toll on their campaign efforts28

With the cost of campaigns increasing drastically – in part as a result of increased vote buying – women’s limited access to financial resources creates barriers to competing in elections. A main source of financial support for any candidate is either personal wealth or support from wealthy patrons, such as businessmen, to whom very few female candidates have access29.

AFGHANISTAN

The problem of lack of access to campaign finance in Afghanistan cannot be understood without acknowledging the deep rooted patriarchal values, unstable political environment and cultural practices prevalent in its society, which shall be explained in detail in the upcoming section. Afghanistan, is also facing serious challenges in terms of gender responsive budgeting because the climate required for bringing forth gender equality is perceived as a western concept and faces resistance from various actors in this process. The fact that there are no allocated budgets to encourage women centric projects like aid for cultivating gardens or aid for women to start small businesses adds to the burden of this image and creates a loop which is difficult to break. Therefore, it is a long way before campaign finance will be funded by the state for women politicians in Afghanistan.

Recent researches have shown that the Ministry of Women Affairs lack seriousness in handling powers vested with this machinery, which often is the cause for not delivering gender equality initiatives.30 The issue of activating government mechanisms can be attempted to resolved by creating networks among activists and civil society participants as well as urban elite to create sex-disaggregated data, capacity building and leadership training.

INDIA

In India, there is no transparency with regards to the source of campaign finance, the way in which it is being spent or any kind of accountability. The cost of 2019 elections was estimated to be almost $8.6 billion, according to the Centre for Media Studies. As politicians splash out on everything from advertising to giant rallies, as well as gifts and transport across the nation of 1.3 billion.31 Traditionally, parties have received funds in cash, cheques, and electoral trusts. But in the beginning of 2017, the government in India made several changes to election contribution rules. A cap on corporate donations to political parties, which banned donations worth more than 7.5 percent of average net profit over three years, was removed. Companies with partial foreign ownership were allowed to donate. And firms were no longer obliged to disclose which parties they were financially backing.

ELECTORAL BONDS & ACCOUNTABILITY

One of the most high-profile changes that affected women and other marginalised communities in politics was the creation of ‘electoral bonds,’ which allowed individuals or companies to deposit funds in a political party’s bank account at the State Bank of India. The donors could purchase as many bonds as they pleased and their identity would not be revealed. Political parties are obligated to declare the amount of money they have received through the bonds, but not the funders’ identity. Political parties are free to spend at will while parliamentary candidates are only allowed to spend up to 7 million rupees (about $100,000) in this election. But lack of effective oversight by the Election Commission of India (ECI) has allowed candidates to flout the limit without much fear of being caught, according to politicians and election experts. The last time a candidate was disqualified for breaking spending rules was in 2011.32

Research also shows that actual spending of candidates from major national parties’ happens through ‘unaccounted funds’, which is as much as eight times the limit. Hoards of cash, liquor and drugs - designed to garner support from voters - seized by the election body during elections is one of the indicators of money spent. Authorities say they have seized goods and cash worth about $456 million since March 26, more than twice the amount seized in the entire 2014 election.33

Therefore, this state of affairs negatively impacts women in politics. Further, in the absence of state funding for campaigns and unregulated contributions from different donors, women who do not have access to public spaces or strong social capital get stuck in a marginalisation loop. The best way to regulate this would be to introduce state funding for women politicians, strengthen existing electoral laws, make the transactions public and ensure accountability in spending by political parties and of course gender responsive budgeting.

GENDER-RESPONSIVE BUDGETING
Now, looking at GRB status in India, the biggest set back is the fact that these measures are done in the form of affirmative action and not by separately allocating a part of the budget for addressing the gender gap. And even in the allocated budgets, they are in the form of public expenditure and leave other crucial actors like NGOs, civil societies as well as ordinary citizens out of their purview. It is noteworthy to mention that a few countries have increasingly attempted greater multi-stakeholder participation (South Africa) with a strong role of civil society and NGOs (Israel, Uganda, UK and Tanzania).34 There aren’t many big budget schemes apart from the Nirbhaya Fund and the Beti Padhao campaign which aims specifically for addressing women related issues. One of the greatest impediments to women trying to enter politics in India is the issue of capital and money required for a successful election rally and this is yet to be reflected in the budget.

SRILANKA

When well-regulated, a strong campaign finance framework will ensure electoral integrity, respect freedom of expression, and encourage the promotion of under-represented groups through equitable access to public campaign financing. It will also counter the abuse of state resources and reduce electoral malpractices such as vote-buying. It is important to understand that lack of regulation in campaign finance does not only affect women in politics, but it also creates an array of other problems like electoral violence, social polarisation, corruption, interference in governance by funders, and misrepresentation. 35

In Sri Lanka, the current electoral system has not included campaign finance regulations since the introduction of the electoral system in 1977. This has compromised the integrity of elections at the national and local levels. The Ceylon (Parliament Elections) Order in Council in 1946 which was the main election law from 1948 to 1970, included adequate provisions to regulate election expenses, excluding contributions. However, with the 1977 electoral reform, campaign finance regulations were completely removed. In the last Presidential election on 16 November of 2019, the common denominator of the national and international election observation reports was the censure on the campaign finance absence36

In the most recent presidential election of 2019, the Centre for Monitoring Election Violence (CMEV) reported that all 35 candidates had spent a combined total of 3796 million LKR (more than 20 million USD) for their election campaigns. The main two candidates had spent 93.86% (3563 million LKR /19 million USD) out of that total estimated expenditure.37However, there was no means for either the public or the Election Commission to demand disclosure of sources and information related to such expenditure that require accountability and transparency.

On the other hand, data about GRB in Sri Lanka reveals that Sri Lanka, with its unhealthy fiscal environment with a total expenditure to GDP ratio much higher than revenue mobilization is in no position to allocate considerable budgetary expenditure for women.38 Due to this, participation of women labour and their presence in the decision making process is much lower in comparison to other South Asian countries like India. One important reason for this is the lack of gender disaggregated data and accountability mechanisms as part of the government’s system. The data available in this area are usually carried out by NGOs, research organisations funded by donors, etc. One way to increase the participation of women in decision making would be to first create a monitoring process like the Gender Budgeting Cell in India.

PAKISTAN

A recently conducted independent study39 interestingly revealed the economic status of Women legislators through data from their assets submitted to the Election Commission of Pakistan from 2002-2013. The findings indicate that strong financial status is an important prerequisite for successful entry into politics. This study found that the majority of elected women belong to an elite class. It is also evident that the common Pakistani woman is still miles away from the corridors of power because she does not have sufficient economic resources to participate in politics. Therefore, in a patriarchal society, even if a woman manages to brave through cultural barriers, access to campaign finance remains an insurmountable problem. Therefore, legislation that increases access to campaign finance for women politicians, especially the marginalised community has to be introduced.

With regards to Gender Responsive Budgeting, Pakistan has had two phases of gender responsive budgeting. In the second phase of gender budgeting from 2008-2012, according to Chakraborty (2016), Pakistan embedded gender-targets in the “output-based budgeting system” across all ministries at the national level. After this second phase, the federal government did some more work on gender-disaggregated analysis and undertook a gender-responsive overview of the budget for FY 2015-16. It led to a baseline to promote gender equality in education and labour force participation with a scope to its applicability at the provincial level.40 For a country struggling with development like Pakistan, in order to increase GRB, the Ministry of Finance should take up the cause to ensure that structural inequalities are evened out through policy reforms.

BANGLADESH

In Bangladesh, election expenses refer to any expenditure incurred or payment made, whether by way of gift, loan, advance, deposit or otherwise, for the arrangement, conduct of, benefit of, or in connection with, or incidental to, the election of a candidate, including the expenditure on account of issuing circulars or publications or otherwise presenting the candidate or his views, aims or objects to voters, but does not include deposits41 The Bangladesh Election Commission (BEC) has prescribed limits on what candidates may expend funds on, e.g., the use of colored posters, cloth banners, or the hiring of vehicles for processions or transporting voters42

However, there is no contribution limit in relation to election candidates apart from political parties. This means that one individual or legal entity could legally contribute the entire amount a candidate is allowed to spend. The absence of regulations on candidate income also means the ban on income from foreign sources that relates to political parties (Representation of the People Order (RPO) 90F (2)) does not apply to candidates. There is, however, an indirect ban on anonymous donations to candidates, as candidates must report all donations received, including the name of the donor43

One way of imposing transparency in Bangladesh is the mandate on submitting a copy of the return to be sent to the BEC by registered post.44 If a candidate fails to submit the return, he or she will face two to seven years imprisonment and a fine45 There are also corresponding provisions to ensure that parties disclose the identity of the campaign funders and show expenditure accounts for the same.

These measures are very important to uphold the spirit of democracy and give a level playing field to candidates participating from different socio-economic backgrounds. But in order to increase the participation of women who have been traditionally placed in a disadvantageous position owing to lack of campaign finance, state funding would be the best practice.

With regards to Gender Responsive Budgeting, Bangladesh46 and Nepal are countries apart from India to have a procedural mechanism for ensuring capacity building, providing information by publishing gender budget statements as a key feature of their gender-responsive budgeting and conducting audits. This should be translated into funding for women in politics as well to eliminate oppression against women.

II. CULTURAL BARRIERS

Political participation as an effort by citizens of the community both individually and in groups to participate in influencing the formation of public policy in a country (Gaffar 1998). Political parties and political figures always try to influence people's attitudes in making political choices and this can increase political participation. The motives of the social actions that underlie the community in participating in politics are different and every community has a motive in making political participation.47 The differences in motives for political participation in society are often influenced by education, employment, social strata. But at the very root of all these choices is culture- which impacts perception, thought process and society’s evolution. Apart from motives, access and ability to effectively participate in politics is to a great extent determined by the socio-cultural climate of any given society. In this section of our report, we shall see the various cultural and societal barriers for entry and effective participation of women politicians.


BANGLADESH

Mastan Culture in Bangladesh- (at the intersection of culture and violence)

Mastan Culture and the Availability of Illegal Arms
Mastan culture and the availability of illegal arms are the most serious hurdles to women's participation in politics. Political parties in Bangladesh rear mastans and promote a widespread mastan culture (Rahman. 1999). This culture also prevents honest, dedicated and competent men from contesting elections. Major political parties of Bangladesh have connections that have access to arms and exercise high influence in the trading world. A study by the Power and Participation Research Centre found that in the 1996 parliamentary election 34% of candidates used mastans. Another report in a daily newspaper reveals that many candidates at the 1996 parliamentary election hired mastans and illegal arms. On 21 August 2004 a grenade attack on an AL meeting in Dhaka left 21 people, including top AL leader Ivy Rahman, dead and over 100 injured. The AL president Sheikh Hasina had a narrow escape. Violence between the BNP and the AL workers continues. In these incidents many people have been injured, and many of them disabled for life. Two lawmakers of the eighth parliament were killed by their political opponents.

Many respondents in the above mentioned interview based research said that they would never win in the election within the dominant mastan culture and the availability of illegal arms, because generally the women of Bangladesh are not involved in this predominantly male culture. Male politicians have a strong network with the mastans, but generally women do not want to link themselves with criminal activities. The violent incidents create a sense of insecurity among women. A woman parliamentarian said: “I am interested in contesting from a general seat in the eighth parliamentary election. But I did not disclose my desire to anyone, because one of my rivals may kill me.” At present most of the Bangladeshi women are not here to take politics as a profession because of the prevailing mastan culture and prevalence of illegal arms. And this, in effect, further strengthens patriarchy.

Marital Status and Age

Women's marital status and age are important factors in engaging in active politics. It is found that all the women parliamentarians elected to the Bangladesh parliament were married except one. Their minimum age was about 35 years. The comment of that one unmarried woman parliamentarian was, “In our society marriage upgrades the status of women, but I was not married due to my political involvement. Generally men do not get married to female politicians. I got many marriage proposals. Some men were not interested to marry me; again some asked me to give up politics. So, I could not get married. Many women are not interested in politics due to the problems of marriage.”

From the comment of this woman parliamentarian, it is apparent that she was dedicated to politics and she did not get a suitable proposal from any one who would agree to allow her to continue her political activities. Kotalova notes, “In Bangladesh it is felt that ‘a woman must be given in marriage at least once’ and more, she should be married ‘in time’” (1996, p. 190). A study reveals that “not only the parents of an unmarried mature girl, but the girl herself, feels guilty if she remains unmarried for some time”. Arranging a suitable marriage is considered more important than becoming established in a career, because some of the major patriarchal elements of the Bangladeshi society do not honour unmarried women, even with high position. One female parliamentarian said: “Men think that unmarried women who come to politics and mix freely with men are promiscuous. Men hope that they can exploit the sexuality of these women.” It is observed that marriage of women active politically is difficult; again there is a great possibility of sexual harassment in the case of unmarried women politicians.

Many women parliamentarians claimed that the age of women is also an important factor in participating in active politics. When a woman becomes older and her children are grown up, she can actively take part in politics. The husband of a women parliamentarian (elected through the by-election from her husband's seat) said, “Women should be involved in politics when they are 40 years old and they have grown up sons and daughters. If they get involved in active politics earlier, then the children will be uncared for and their house will be dirty”. Another female member revealed that there was a great possibility of sexual harassment in the case of young women politicians, because “men's attraction to young and beautiful girls was natural”.

Again marriage is helpful for women politicians. It is found that 18 out of 23 women interviewed got the position of Member of Parliament because of their husbands' political connections. Sometimes widows of politically-connected persons or politicians get involved in politics.

Bangladesh is also one of the few of the countries that recognized the sacrifice of women by honouring the widows and rape victims as ‘Birangana’ or ‘war heroines’ (Sengupta, 2014) and later adopting a private member’s resolution in the parliament in 2015 to recognize them as freedom fighters (New Age, 2015).

However, exactly when Bangladesh was starting to get recognition for empowering women in the political domain, the government decided to amend the Child Marriage Restraint Act 1929. On 27 February 2017, the state minister for MoWCA placed the Child Marriage Restraint Bill (2017) in the parliament which contains a special provision: ‘if… marriage takes place in line with the directives of… court and consent of… guardians… in special context… in best interest of an underage female… it will not be considered as an offence.’ This reversed 46 years of attempts at empowering women in the country. The state minister of MoWCA also stated:

“The law aims at reducing marriage of girls under 15 to zero by 2021 and cut the rate by two-thirds in case of 15–18 age groups. It also aims to free Bangladesh from child marriage by 2041 as promised by Prime Minister in the Girl Summit 2014 in Britain.”

Interestingly, although before-15 years child-marriage rate is 18 per cent, the percentage of before-18 years marriage is 52 per cent in Bangladesh (UNICEF, 2016). Hence, whether this is a strategy for the government to achieve child marriage reduction goal may be a possible argument. But, it certainly is not a desired gender-equitable policy.

PAKISTAN

Misinterpretation of Religion- Purdah and Pakthunwali

According to the findings of the Gender Social Norms Index report, released recently by the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP), the percentage of those holding at least one sexist bias was the most in Pakistan, at 99.81%. The index measured how social beliefs act as a hindrance to gender equality in areas like politics, work and education. On the other hand, The World Economic Forum has ranked Pakistan at 151 out of 153 countries in its 2020 Global Gender Gap Index. Recently Pakistan also witnessed one of its largest ever women rights demonstrations in the country pointing towards the very reality brought out by the two reports.

The lack of women participation in politics in Pakistan can be linked to the old fashioned mindset, which views women as incapable of handling leadership positions. This mindset considers women unfit for other productive roles, thus limiting them only to domestic chores within the four walls. Women’s equality in terms of political participation is hindered by customs and traditions. Women in Pakistan are restrained by age-old customs and traditions that limit their involvement and contribution to the public sphere. The constitution of Pakistan places no restriction on women’s participation in the political sphere, however their presence in parties as well as institutions at the various levels remain insignificant.

In a recent study conducted in Balochistan in 2016 48, women were asked why they do not enter the political sphere and support the men. The chief reasons for the same were patriarchal domination and misunderstanding of Islam. These redundant conditions are attributed to the rigid customs prevalent in the Pakistani society. Purdah as well as concerns of women’s honour also directly impact their political empowerment. The cultural norms of Pakistan’s society promote the segregation and seclusion of women, often justified through the use of religion, which is at times misinterpreted.. For example, religiously formulated positions that argue for gender segregation and female seclusion (Purdah) limits women’s access to resources in general; restrict from the simple act of casting vote and as well as participating in politics (Shaheed et al, 2009:26-29).

Likewise, religion is used as an instrument of stereotype against women political aspirants. For instance, the Purdah system prevents women from participating in politics either as voters, aspirants or even participating in campaigns/other electoral activities. Some communities also boycott women's participation in politics saying it is un-Islamic. They were of the opinion that leadership of societies is men's job as per their religion, and women must be responsible for the household chores.

The Pakhtun code of life includes principles of living attributed to men and women, focusing on the desired appropriate behaviour. In this regard according to Pakhtun code of conduct, politics is a public process requiring high mobility for interaction and constant public appearance while cultural norms of Pakthunwali promote the segregation and seclusion of women often justified through the use of religion.

Women political participation is considered a landmark in women empowerment and in Pakistan there are various barriers deflecting women participation irrespective of the reserved quota given. In such exceedingly patriarchal and delicate scenarios the exercise of women’s political action even with the quotas in place can be highly challenging. Thus, the struggle for women in politics continues.49

SRI LANKA

Gender stereotyping and the proxy women

Prabhashini Wickaramsinghe is a Councillor at Moratuwa Municipal Council, and was elected in 2018. She is a full time political activist associated with the JVP party. She said “ We celebrate 8th March as International Women’s Day but the problems faced by women remain the same. The society expects that the only responsibility of a woman should be looking after her husband, kids and family and that women who engage in politics can’t lead successful family lives. When I decided to engage in politics, my parents did not agree. They worried that politics is not suitable for a girl and what society would think of me. But I stood up for myself and they understood my intentions after sometime.”

In 2019, the female representation in the national parliament in Sri Lanka consisted of 5.33 percent of the available seats. This was a slight decrease from the previous year, in which females held 5.8 percent of the available seats in the national parliament in Sri Lanka. The current parliament only has 12 female legislators out of 225 members. Men and women in Sri Lanka are socialised to see politics pragmatically as the arena of men rather than women. While the public political sphere is for men, women’s primary role is considered to be within the household as wives, mothers and housekeepers. This gender stereotyping and the creation as well as idealising of the ‘good woman’ is internalised through the educational institutions and media. This leads to a partition between the spheres of involvement. Due to which women tend to lack interest and confidence to participate in politics and don’t think of it as an activity for themselves (Matland 2005: 93-94, Jayawardena and Kodikara 2003, Thiruchandran 1998).

BHUTAN

Gendered division of labour and Hierarchical practices

Bhutan is the land of Gross National Happiness (GNH), where the majority of the population is female, and matrilineal customs are followed.

In 2018, Of the 188 people in the 47 constituencies from four political parties in the primary election for the National Assembly, only 19 are women, taking the women candidate percentage to 10.1 percent.

Women’s political status was not recognised even under the monarchy as not a single woman was invited to become a minister despite the fact that the King appointed the ministers until 1997. The role of women in the government did not change after devolution of power to the cabinet from 1998 to 2007 (Gallenkamp 2010:10).

Bhutanese women’s participation in the public sphere and representation at the decision-making and leadership positions is extremely limited and women continue to be relegated to the domestic and private sphere.

Dorji et al. (2012)’s research on Bhutan’s Local Government elections asserted that irrespective of gender, capability of the candidate is seen as the most important characteristic of a probable candidate. However, voters showed a small degree of preference for female candidates. This is attributed to the traditional view that “women are homemakers and that society has less confidence in them for public positions” (Dorji et al. 2012: 60). Absence of confidence among women is stated as the main factor. The two other stated reasons were illiteracy and status of women in society (Dorji et al. 2012: 60).

Confinement of women in the home along with the educational requirements for parliamentary participation continue to exclude women from active political life. The low level of female literacy and the low number of women in university indicates that politics is specially for men.

The underlying issue in Bhutan is that the societal mindset is driven by patriarchal ideology of division of labour where men are seen as breadwinners and women as homemakers. Such views continue to disadvantage women’s empowerment and development.

Age old customs reinforces patriarchy and people continue to relate attributes such as superiority and authority with men. Further, women are not well received if found playing the traditional khuru – a game similar to darts. A pair of darts is shot at the target which is set about 10 metres from the players. Traditionally khuru is associated with men. Recently women started playing khuru during their leisure time and at special local festivals. People in general blamed women playing khuru for natural disasters, pointing towards their devotion to ideologies of patriarchy. Women, therefore continue to be restricted to the private sphere as their participation in games and sports is also frowned upon.

Women are often referred to with the respectable term “nangi aum”, literally meaning, inner madam. This term directs to the very gendered division of labour where women are relegated to the household or the domestic space.

Bhutanese society has ascribed rules for females and these draw on the idea of women’s inferiority. The role of the women of Bhutan is therefore dependent on the scaffolding of a patriarchal society, its impact on the way work can be divided on the basis of gender.

Despite women marking their place in areas such as education, health and employment in Bhutan and guarantees of gender equality in the Constitution, they have made a poor showing in electoral politics.51

NEPAL

Quantitative representation but not qualitative

As per Ian Martin, the head of the UN Mission to Nepal (UNMIN-2006 to 2009),

‘At all the political negotiating tables I have seen in Nepal during the peace process, not once have I seen a woman at the table.’

Despite a lot of uproar by the women’s movement, neither the Maoists nor the political parties representing the government included any female representatives to the peace talks. The negotiations therefore were highly male dominated.

Even the committee set up to develop a new Interim Constitution, was devoid of women at the time of its formation. After considerable pressure and push it led to the inclusion of four women and one representative from the Dalit community. During the process of constitution making, women from different arenas pressured for guaranteed women’s representation in the new political institutions that were to be established. Their voices were heard, and an affirmative action clause for women’s political representation was incorporated.

In this view, a Nepal NGO Report on Beijing +15 (2009) states, “

“Even though there has been some numerical improvement in the quantitative dimension as a result of the affirmative action/positive discrimination, the overall participation of women in decisionmaking and powerful positions is still very low.” In addition, a report by South Asia Partnership (SAP, 2009) International proclaims ,

“Because of quantitative reservation and proportional participation the number of women representation in the political structure has sequentially increased. However, corresponding qualitative improvements in women’s participation is yet to happen.”

Women’s participation in decision making is obstructed by male domination present inside the political parties. As decisions in political parties continue to be made by a few senior and high caste male leaders, and women's status as well as authority in the political parties are determined on the basis of their relation and kinship to particular leaders, thereby leaving their capabilities and talent aside. A female politician shared her views on the same,

“Nepal is still a maledominated society. There’s a tendency of leaders to think that women aren’t as capable as men. So they bring in more men in their party. There are statements from parties to say that in principle they are inclusive of women, but they haven’t been practising what they talk about.”

Another issue that impacts women’s participation is the ideology that the inclusion of women representatives was in response to legal requirements, i.e the quota which was set up. It was not the determination of the parties themselves to improve gender equality in political scenarios and decision making.

The 2017 local elections, which were held for the very first time in almost two decades, resulted in 40 percent of women’s representation in local governments. Though this can be referred to as a historic moment, however these outcomes were heavily aided by legislated gender quotas that promised 40 percent female representation at ward membership level and at least one female candidate for either mayor/chief or deputy mayor/chief position at municipality level, rural as well. Consequently, women who were situated in close proximity to men with political power benefited the most.

The underlying issue to this is that quotas have increased the inclusion of women but this has been done on the cost of minority group men, not majority group men (Dalit vis a vis Khas Arya men). Therefore women’s inclusion cannot be understood as a change in the existing power structures, rather it may lead to other forms of marginalisation.

Even though women candidates may have personal connections, but that did not safeguard them from gender and caste-based discrimination which they faced even within their parties as well as the society.

Talking about the difference between women and men politicians, a female party worker said – “Women do politics drinking tea, while men do politics drinking alcohol.”

This further directs us to the idea of “doing” politics which requires pacifying cadres as well as discussing various ideas and policies, which leads to the advantages such as alcohol and money in the hands of the men. Alcohol drinking takes place at certain times and specific places, these spaces are gendered, leaving women out of the narrative completely due to lack of social acceptance.

Nepal as a society is characterized by a strong patriarchal culture, where women have been marginalized from participating in public life. The society in itself is complexly heterogenous and therefore the social status and equality with men varies as per the various ethnic groups, regions and castes. This patriarchal hierarchy is seen in the norms and practices of Nepal, such as Chaupadhi Pratha, a practice whereby women are secluded to cow sheds during their menstruation period. Other being citizenship rights (women in Nepal are unable to pass their citizenship onto their children) and inheritance rights. Their view is that women are subordinate to men and their role should be confined to the domestic sphere, which leads to creation of negative social stereotypes about women as dependent, passive, submissive, domestic which have been internalised at different levels.

Gender quotas are important and necessary but to transform the system, interventions are required focused on creating socio-economic opportunities for women, equalising the unequal gendered division of labour, breaking the stereotypes, as well as the patriarchal institutions including political parties. While women’s rights have come a long way in Nepal, dynamics around elections, with gender-inclusive quotas in place, reveal the patriarchal mindset and hierarchies that further lead to women’s inequality. 52

In Nepal, the system of development administration is decentralized to the Village Development Committee (VDC) and municipality levels, which comprise several wards. VDCs consist of nine wards, while the number of wards in the municipalities ranges from 9 to 34. The political structure of the district and village development committee is formed. Each DDC also must have at least one woman nominated for it. Ensuring women’s participation at the grassroots level alone is not adequate to representing 50 percent of the population. INDIA

Gender discrimination, deeming women invisible

Women’s status in electoral politics and governance is far below the desired representative levels, as it is viewed as the playground of men, with not only few women struggling to enter Parliament.

The number of female representatives at the Centre and in most of the states of India is below 20%, showcasing a gendered exclusion from electoral participation as well as lack of quality representation. The Indian female participants in the electoral politics are mainly less because of political party competition, as national and regional political parties discriminate not only in terms of seat allotments, but also in the chain of command. As the party competition structure in the Indian politics is ruled by inherent male dominance and a patriarchal mindset that excludes women from the electoral process. Parties are usually not in favour of nominating women candidates from seats seen as “winnable”. Often women are granted candidature from uncertain seats where the party is not too confident of its chances.

Such insecure mindsets, tangled in the webs of patriarchy pursue the notion of women taking their place as leaders and decision-makers, thereby eventually representing their issues and interests instead of leaning on men to do so. The legislators or the people in power are influenced by the age old rules and customs of male domination which is evident through the misogynistic remarks made against women leaders which clearly asserts that politics remains an unequal battleground for women. MALDIVES

Some of the cultural barriers that exist in Maldives include dominance of men55, negative self-perceptions of women, stereotyping in textbooks56, lack of confidence in women in assuming leadership roles by both men and women57, lack of public space for women and discrimination.58 Maldives has a culture of men congregating at ‘hotas,’ or traditional men’s cafes where they discuss politics/business. Women have no likewise spaces to increase their social capital.59 And less than 4% of men contribute to household tasks and childcare60 and there is no government support for child care, it becomes the sole responsibility of the women. Therefore, uncompensated labour at home helps the exploitative economy to thrive and creates insurmountable barriers for women to enter into politics.

Several surveys and focus group discussions conducted by other implementers in the Maldives showed there was a lack of support for women’s equality. The Democracy at the Crossroads survey conducted by Transparency Maldives (TM) in 2013 found that about two-thirds of the public do not support the idea of gender equality. A human rights survey conducted by the Human Rights Commission of the Maldives (HRCM) in 2005 and again in 2011 depicted a decline in men’s belief in women’s equality. When looking in the aggregate at responses to questions pertaining to women’s rights, HRCM found that “men have become more conservative on issues related to women’s rights…whereas women’s views, for the most part, have altered much less and in some areas are more strongly supportive of women’s rights.”61

HRCM’s survey findings also illustrated the dominance of men in society. A significant percentage of respondents indicated that they considered women to have a subordinate role in the husband/wife relationship; 87.3 percent of women said that they agreed or strongly agreed that a good wife always obeys her husband even if she disagrees.62 Based on anecdotal information gathered from those consulted during this assessment, this often translates into a husband expecting a wife to vote the way he instructs. Negative public and self-perceptions of women are reinforced by stereotyping of gender roles in school textbooks, i.e. portraying women as cooking and cleaning, with girls helping, while conversely portraying boys playing active games and men as heroes.

Other surveys showed that some women were not supportive of other women taking elected leadership roles. UNDP’s Women in Public Life 2011 focus group discussions showed mixed views on women running for political office. While some male and female respondents said that they would support women entering the political arena (predominantly in the southern atolls), many urban men and women expressed traditional views of women’s roles in the family, demonstrating that they would likely not vote for a female candidate.63 TM’s Democracy at the Crossroads survey results showed that 70% of women believe that women make worse leaders than men while only 60% of men responded to have felt this way, highlighting the pervasive impact of internalised patriarchy and the need for gender empowerment training.

UNDP’s Women in Public Life study found that women lack confidence in their ability to function in a leadership position. Female participants perceived “courage and willingness to carry responsibility” as important qualities for a successful politician, and many of these participants felt that women might not have these qualities.64 Those consulted during IFES’ in-country interviews said that women’s lack of confidence in general, specifically in public speaking, affects their interest in running for office.

In Afghanistan, it is important to understand the historical and cultural context of the word “democracy” and associated understandings of democratic processes, such as elections. VIOLENCE AGAINST WOMEN IN POLITICS/ELECTIONS (VAWP/E)

What does the term “violence against women in politics/elections” mean?

Violence against women in elections and politics (VAWE/P) refers to types of violence that exist in the exercise of political competition and governance in (at least nominally) democratic states and during democratization processes.66Violence against women in politics is understood as the supra-category encompassing violence that takes place outside the direct electoral process but within the context of peace-time politics. VAWE is, as the name suggests, restricted to violence directly connected to an electoral process. According to the National Democratic Institute (NDI), violence against women in politics encompasses all forms of aggression, coercion and intimidation against women as political actors simply because they are women.67 These forms of violence and harassment don’t garner public attention, either because they take place in private spaces or because, due to prevailing gender based stereotypes and prejudices , they are accepted.

IFES defines the broad umbrella of violence against women in elections (VAWE) as “Any harm or threat of harm committed against women with the intent and/or impact of interfering with their free and equal participation in the electoral process during the electoral period. It includes harassment, intimidation, physical harm or coercion, threats, and financial pressures, and it may be committed in the home or other private spaces, or in public spaces. These acts may be directed at women in any of their roles as electoral stakeholders (e.g. voters, media, political actors, state actors, community leaders, or electoral officials)68

One of the most significant barriers to women in politics is the threat as well as actual use of violence and is seen as a common feature in South Asia. The violent nature of politics within South Asia often deters women from participating within the political sphere69. Studies point that candidates and their families as well as voters have routinely faced violence during elections. It is important to begin by understanding that politics has also traditionally been promoted as the territory of men. When women try to enter the area of decision making by defying patriarchal norms, they face various forms of violence that includes casual sexism, harassment, bias, discrimination, psychological and physical violence. Further, social and cultural norms; inferior economic status; election violence, assault on character/reputation; low literacy rates and educational levels, unequal access to education; difficulty on obtaining nomination – male preference by parties for nomination; religious norms disabling women to break away from traditional roles and lack of encouragement from women already in politics are the deterrents to women’s participation in politics in South Asia.70

Violence against women in politics (VAWP) is violence that occurs within the political sphere but that specifically targets women. VAWP is used to reinforce traditional social and political structures by targeting women leaders who challenge patriarchy and the prevailing social expectations and norms. It restricts women’s mobility and capacity to participate within the political sphere. VAWP has been defined71 as:

“The connotation of any act of violence that results in, or is likely to result in physical, sexual or psychological harm or suffering to women politicians, including threat of such acts, coercion or arbitrary deprivation of liberty in public or private life.”

VAMP is one of the most cruel forms of violence because apart from being an apparent device of inequality, it is used to maintain the subordinate position of women in society and shun them away from public decision making spaces. Drawing from her experience in India, Dr. Indra Biseswar argues that gender norms are generally still rigid in many communities, especially rural ones, when it comes to women in leadership positions. Women running for political office defy social norms and are considered ‘intruders’ of a space not meant for them. For this, they are often scrutinized, stigmatized and targeted even during their campaigns or other visits to their constituencies72. It is pertinent to note that although men in politics also face violence, the motives and the impact of VAMP are different for women. In the latter, in some cases, it manifests as a warped sense of righteousness by patriarchal forces. By sending a grisly message of warning to other women, they are deterred from entering this arena. This threatens the representation of women in politics and by extension, equality and democracy itself. The following comparative data for South Asia looks at various measures taken to tackle the problem of VAWIP and provide suggestions/best practices to ensure a safe space for women.

MANIFESTATIONS OF VIOLENCE - it includes what are the types like physical, psychological, threats, etc., Manifestations of violence towards politically active women like legislators, campaigners or voters are classified in several common categories – psychological, physical, sexual and economic – all of whom present distinctive features when applied to political life in a democracy.

AFGHANISTAN

In Afghanistan, there is a history of violence surrounding elections with the Taliban targeting candidates, voters, poll booths, etc. There is always a sense of insecurity looming around the political space for everyone and especially, women. In Afghanistan, it is important to understand the historical and cultural context of the word “democracy” and associated understandings of democratic processes, such as elections. Contemporary research has found that the term “democracy” was associated not just with certain political processes, but also with a series of practices that were considered un-islamic.73At the same time, Afghan men were more likely than women to associate democracy with the behaviour of women, suggesting some problematic conclusions about the opposition of men to female participation in democratic activities like elections, decision making, etc. It is also pertinent to note that violence as a form of political violence takes different forms and its objective most often is to influence election outcomes74 and distribution of political power. In other spheres, violence against women in positions of power is instituted to muffle their presence in decision making or public places like Malalai Kakar, the highest-ranking female police officer who was killed in 2008, allegedly because she was leading a unit of ten policewomen dealing with domestic violence. In a similar manner, Sitara Achakzai, was also killed for her activism for womens’ rights. But, there are no laws in Afghanistan that prohibits women from participating in politics or jobs, it in fact encourages women participation. There are also special Independent Election Commission regulations to provide registration and polling centres for women. However, the real challenge is in implementation.These provisions are well intentioned but remain unenforced by virtue of deeply rooted prejudices and power imbalances in the Afghani society.

In 2009, Law on Elimination of Violence against Women75 was enacted in accordance with Article 24 and 54 of Afghanistan’s Constitution76 which aims to protect the liberty of human beings and physical and psychological well being of family, especially of child and mother respectively. It is pertinent to note that one of the objectives of this legislation is maintaining family integrity and fighting against customs, traditions and practices which contradict Islamic Sharia and cause violence against women. However, keeping in mind our earlier explanation for how democratic practices are perceived in Afghanistan, a huge abyss exists between this legislation and protecting violence against women in politics in execution.

There are provisions in this legislation for promoting relevant training by NGOs/other organisations to educate people and protect women against violence. Under Article 15 of this Act, a special body named “Elimination of violence against Women” High Commission under the Ministry of Women Affairs was commissioned. It details the roles and responsibilities of different departments in prevention and protection of violence against women. The Chapter- III of this act extensively deals with criminal provisions for different forms of violence against women and the corresponding punishments. Hence, some of the key suggestions would be to implement the existing laws in a stringent manner, increase visibility of women in politics through feminist media, increase participation and capacity building of women in politics, gender sensitisation and awareness campaigns for rights of women.

PAKISTAN

In Pakistan, VAW is generally considered to be a law and order issue where hierarchies among crimes are created and accepted as normal. Prioritizing sexual violence in comparison to other forms of violence gives the message that some forms of violence are accepted.77 This also leads to the neglect of other forms of violence the women face. Considering VAWP to be of low importance and turning a blind eye towards incidences where women are deprived of their rights in their public and private lives has challenged growth.There is also some stigma attached to going to the police station (even to register a complaint) in Pakistan.78

Further, studies indicate that religious extremism, which has its interconnection with social divisions and power relations cannot be ignored when understanding VAWP. Particularly for Pakistan it is critical to take into consideration provincial or local specificities when analysing VAWP. These local differences can influence the forms of VAWP, the characteristics of perpetrators of VAWP and the community’s perception or ideas relating to VAWP within each province/state.

With that being explained, owing to the rise of religious fundamentalism in Pakistan, the Hudood Ordinance was introduced to implement Islamic Sharia by enforcing punishment, mentioned in the Quran. This has led to many incidences where women subjected to rape/gang rape were eventually accused of zina (extramarital sex) and incarcerated.79

Certain other legislations like Qisas or Dyat80 are the direct legacy of the military dictatorship and rely on conservative principles from the public and political sphere. The Qisas and Dyat Acts elevate elements of traditional justice to statutory law, encouraging out-of-court settlements in accordance with local customs often involving monetary compensation. The result has been that such killings seldom lead to conviction and imprisonment.

However, there are several laws in place for protection of violence against women and ensuring equality. Under Article 25 of the Constitution of Pakistan81, equality of citizens is guaranteed and no one is to be discriminated on the basis of their sex. The country also has Constitutional provisions that guarantee equality for women through Articles 32, 35, 51, 140A82. The Representation of People Act (RoPA) also includes quotas for women representation whilst the introduction of Local Government Ordinance (LGO) Act in 2000 mandated for 33% representation of women in local level politics. This has been paralleled by other pro-women legislation aimed at addressing widespread gender inequality and discrimination.

Recently, under the leadership of the first female Speaker of the National Assembly, the first-ever bipartisan Caucus of Women Parliamentarians has enabled its members to work jointly for the cause of women in Pakistan. The Caucus has focused its efforts on policies and services for women, especially women survivors of violence. This has been reflected in the introduction of hotline services to provide access to medical, legal and security assistance in one phone call. Through the Caucus, attention is also being focused on the plight of acid and kerosene burn victims, their treatment and rehabilitation. Funds are being dedicated to the construction of burn centres, particularly in remote and underdeveloped areas, and campaigning is underway for empowering women survivors traditionally marginalized and excluded from society.83 However, the rhetoric which promotes participation of women in politics and the creation of safe spaces for them will remain an empty promise without proper policy implementation.

NEPAL

While physical violence, verbal abuse and threat of violence are higher for India, character assassination is seen as a greater threat in Nepal. This country has a strict law in place where the Constitution of Nepal, 2015 has made it mandatory that there should be 33 per cent women in both the federal and State parliaments. Additionally, the Election Commission (EC) has obliged the political parties to implement this provision, which has greatly enhanced the women’s access to the parliament. But this does not mean that it is a women- friendly political atmosphere. In her book, Women and Politics in the Third World (2005), Haleh Ashfer states ‘masculinisation’ of politics is a historical fact. She argues that men have to provide for their families and consequently anything remotely to do with power falls in their space, which is predominantly public. Ms. Chitralekha Yadav, the former Deputy Speaker of Nepal Pratinidhi Sabha expressed her concern over the dominant role played by the 3Ms (money, muscle and mafia) and the 3Gs (gold, guns and goondas or mobsters) in politics of South Asia.84

Some of the violence here includes denial of the right to vote, compelling to vote, denial of the right to contest elections, denial of access to resources, side- lining, denial of education and especially political education, denial of easy access to polling station, lack of women staff in women polling stations, threats, kidnapping and killing were some other forms of violence. The Criminal Code of Nepal has provisions dealing with violence against women but it does not have a legislation specifically addressing VAWP. It is noteworthy to mention a truly novel mechanism devised by South Asia Partnership International, a NGO based in Nepal that came up with a Surveillance System85 in 2006 to monitor/prevent and issue guidelines for dealing with VAWP.

INDIA

In India, studies show that socio-economic divide and existing power structures are the main reasons for VAMIP in India. For women living here, violence or the risk of violence permeates every aspect of their lives from birth to death. It is estimated that one third of South Asian women experience violence throughout their lives.86 VAW is institutionalised through family structures, wider social and economic frameworks and cultural and religious traditions. It is a widely accepted method for controlling women. Moreover, it is largely overlooked by law enforcement agencies and is ignored by those in power .This violence becomes particularly abhorrent when inflicted upon women in power.

1 in every 7 tweets that mentioned women politicians in India was 'problematic' or 'abusive' 13.8% of the tweets that mentioned 95 women politicians in the study were problematic (10.5%) or abusive (3.3%). This amounted to 1 million problematic or abusive mentions of the 95 women between March and May 2019, or over 10,000 problematic or abusive tweets every day across all women in the sample.Online abuse has the power to belittle, demean, intimidate and eventually silence women.87

Gender inequality remains a salient issue in India, which ranks 135 out of 187 on the Gender Inequality Index. In 2018, India was ranked the most dangerous country in the world for women and in recent months, violence against women has resurged. While last year’s election ushered in a record-high level of women to India’s lower house, political spheres continue to be dominated by men, who today make up 86% of the house’s representatives.88

The Constitution of India89: Article 14, 16 ensure equality of status, Article 15 says state shall not discriminate among any citizen on the ground of sex, religion etc. Other legislations in place to protect women include CEDAW, ICCPR, UDHR .Several articles are incorporated in the Indian constitution in order to materialise the desired objective of equality and justice for the women in India. Besides the preamble, the articles of the constitution, which impinge on our subject of enquiry are Articles 14,15,16 under part 111, Articles 39, 42, 44 under part IV, Article 5 1 - A(c) under part IV A and Article 246 under part XI are noteworthy. Participation of women in Panchayati Raj institutions has been an area of much interest in India. Participation in the local government institutions has been viewed as essential in promoting women's consciousness and development at the local level as well as in training them for participation in wider politics. 90 Thus,measures are being taken to bridge the gap between men and women in politics however there is a long way to go before such disparities disappear.

BHUTAN

It has only been in the recent times that Bhutan has transformed from monarchy to democracy and the challenges faced by women are unique to this set up. Based on the state’s definition of diverse politics, women party supporters mainly participated in women’s wings during the first democratic elections in 2008. However, women’s wings were less effective and remained outside the mainstream Drukpa male directed politics. The ECB stopped the women’s wing from campaigning separately in the latter part of the campaign in the 2008 elections. So the women of these parties started functioning as different women's support groups and worked outside its party office. According to the Party’s Secretary who managed the party’s administration, the DPT women’s support group is thought to be the Party President’s wife’s personal initiative to address women’s wellbeing. The group’s representative, mostly the Prime Minister’s wife, attended party executive meetings and the general assembly and informed the party office about its programme through reports. The ordinary women were not included in the party politics. Therefore, women’s separate status within the DPT and PDP’s actual politics (the only two political parties in Bhutan) reveals that they were excluded from the mainstream politics and their issues were sidelined. They were seen to be given a role in politics but it was neither central nor important and contributed to an apparent veneer of equality while the main party and its political work remained firmly in male hands. As the kickstart to women in politics began like this, women who wished to enter mainstream politics faced backlash.

Some of the laws/measures/interventions in place apart from international conventions to protect the rights of women in Bhutan are as follows91:

The 2008 Constitution under Article 7 (15) states that: “All persons are equal before the law and are entitled to equal and effective protection of the law and shall not be discriminated against on the grounds of race, sex, language, religion, politics or other status.”

The 11th Five-Year Plan (2013-2018) established “Gender Friendly Environment for Women’s Participation” as one of the 16 National Key Result Areas, with “Draft legislation to ensure quotas for women in elected offices, including the Parliament and local government bodies'' as one of the key performance indicators. Based on the recommendations given by UN Convention on Elimination of All forms of Discrimination Against Women (CEDAW), The National Plan of Action to Promote Gender Equality in Elected Office (NPAPGEEO) was developed as a temporary special measure. It identified women in politics and provided necessary capacity building support to create a level playing field. Another noteworthy and welcome measure is the Thimpu Declaration of 201792, during which the following goals were laid down,

Goal 2 (2020-2021): Increase the number of women elected local leaders by 30 percent using fast track measures.
Goal 3: Increase the number of women executives and leaders in civil services/ public service by 25 percent by 2025

An important Civil Society Organisation named RENEW worked very closely with the NAWC Committee by drafting, reviewing, lobbying with political parties and passed the Domestic Violence Prevention Bill in July 2012. The NCWC and RENEW’s partnership backed by civil society actors from the Tarayana Foundation, and other actors helped to exert pressure on the male dominated National Assembly to pass the Domestic Violence Prevention Bill. However, there is no legislation to specifically deal with VAMIP. Other grassroot NGOs which have worked closely with improving participation of women in politics is Bhutan Network for Empowering Women. (BNEW)

The forms of VAWIP in Bhutan is observed through sidelining of women from mainstream politics, verbal harassments, threats, etc as well as the absence of data regarding physical violence against women in politics.These indicators are a manifestation of an entrenched patriarchal structure that aims to muffle the voices of women in Bhutanese society.

BANGLADESH

Through a study96 conducted by IFES, Bangladesh’s pervasive violent political culture hampers women’s political participation. Women are afraid of becoming victims of political attacks and clashes. Women also fear their families limiting their participation in political activity.

  • Sexual violence (as a form of electoral violence) has been experienced through harassment and assault of women candidates, political activists and voters.

  • Some women are perpetrators of electoral violence. Within the home, they are using intimidation to limit voter choice. In the public sphere, they are slandering other candidates. SRILANKA

In a recent research conducted by the OHCHR, it was found out that VAWIP was tied to domestic violence and perceived marital/family obligations.
